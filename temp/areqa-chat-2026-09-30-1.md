Welcome! Let’s break down this paper together step by step. We will start with the intuitive picture of what an "aerial river" is, define what makes a basin "critical," and then see how it impacts rainfall downwind.

---

### Step 1: The Intuition — What is an "Aerial River"?

Think of an aerial river (often called a "flying river") as an invisible river running through the atmosphere. 

* In a normal surface river, water flows down slopes into oceans or lakes.
* In an **aerial river**, water is evaporated or transpired into the sky by upwind forests and vegetation (**evapotranspiration**), carried across long distances by prevailing atmospheric winds, and finally released as **precipitation** in downwind regions.

---

### Step 2: What is a "Critical Upwind Basin"?

When researchers trace where a downwind city's rain comes from, the total moisture source area (sometimes called an *atmospheric basin* or *precipitationshed*) can be massive. For example, tracing all moisture to Pakistan can stretch from Scandinavia all the way to Madagascar. Trying to protect an entire continent is simply impractical for land management.

To solve this, the authors identify the **critical upwind basin**:

> **Definition:** For any given region, its **critical upwind basin** is the specific core upwind area that supplies moisture most efficiently via aerial rivers before reaching a mathematical "diminishing return" point.

The authors determine this boundary by plotting a **moisture drainage curve** (Basin Area vs. Cumulative Moisture Contribution) and locating its mathematical **turning point**:
* **Before the turning point:** Adding a relatively small area of upwind land gives you a huge fraction of downwind rainfall.
* **After the turning point:** You have to include massive amounts of land for only tiny, negligible additions of moisture.

The upwind area prior to this turning point is designated as the **critical upwind basin**.

### Step 3: How Does It Impact the Rainfall System?

The paper reveals two primary mechanisms linking critical upwind basins to rainfall:

1. **Direct Moisture Supply and Land-Use Feedback:**
   Because aerial rivers rely on evapotranspiration from upwind vegetation (especially dense canopies like the Amazon), changes in land use directly alter the moisture load in the air. 
   * *Concrete Example:* The paper notes that deforestation in the upwind Peruvian Ucayali region can cause a **5–13% reduction in annual rainfall** and a **19–50% decrease in annual river runoff** downwind. If the critical upwind basin is degraded, the aerial river runs dry, reducing rainfall in downwind agricultural lands and cities.

2. **Regional Variations Across Four Regimes (G1 to G4):**
   The authors classify South American regions into four drainage types based on their position along the aerial river path:
   * **G1 (Headwater regions, e.g., windward Amazon, coastal Chile):** Receive mostly oceanic moisture. Their critical upwind basins contribute 90–95% of their terrestrial moisture, but terrestrial moisture makes up only ~12% of total rainfall.
   * **G2 (Drainage zones, e.g., mid-Amazon):** Aerial rivers carry their maximum moisture load here. Critical basins supply 60–75% of continental-sourced rain (~20–25% of total rainfall).

Therefore, interventions in their critical upwind basins yield the highest relative payoff per unit area for regional water security.

Let’s demystify the turning point. It can look intimidating with all the calculus notation, but the core intuition is simple: **it is the mathematical point where you hit "diminishing returns."**

Here is how the authors identify it step by step.

---

### Step 1: The Intuition — Sorting by "Efficiency"

Imagine you want to buy moisture for your downwind city, and you want the biggest bang for your buck:

1. The authors take a map of the entire upwind continent and divide it into grid cells (pixels).
2. For every grid cell, they calculate: **"How much rain does this specific patch of land send to our target city?"**
3. They rank every grid cell from **highest contributor** to **lowest contributor**.
4. They start adding the cells together, one by one, starting from the most productive patch.

---

### Step 2: The Curve — What Does It Look Like?

When you plot this cumulative process on a graph, you get the **Moisture Drainage Curve**:

* **X-axis ($A$):** Cumulative upwind land area added (starting from the best cell, then the 2nd best, etc.).
* **Y-axis ($F(A)$):** Cumulative moisture delivered to your target region.

```text
Cumulative Moisture F(A)
       ^
       |                      ---------------- (Flattening out: tiny gains)
       |                 . - ' 
       |              .-'  <-- TURNING POINT (Maximum "bend")
       |            .' 
       |          .' 
       |        .' 
       |       /   (Steep slope: huge gains per square km)
       |      /
       |     /
       +---------------------------------------------> Upwind Area (A)
```

* **At the start (steep slope):** Every square kilometer you protect adds a massive amount of moisture downwind.
* **At the end (flat slope):** You are adding huge tracts of land thousands of miles away that only send an occasional drop of rain.

The **turning point** is the "elbow" or "knee" of this curve—the exact boundary between high-yield upwind land and low-yield upwind land.

---

### Step 3: The Math — How Do They Calculate It?

To find the exact elbow without guessing visually, the authors use **differential geometry** (calculating the **curvature**, denoted $\kappa$):

1. **First Derivative ($F'(A)$):** This is the slope (the moisture rate per unit area). Because we sorted from highest contributor to lowest, the slope is continuously decreasing.
2. **Curvature Formula ($\kappa(A)$):**
   $$\kappa(A) = \frac{|F''(A)|}{\left(1 + [F'(A)]^2\right)^{3/2}}$$
   * In plain English: **Curvature measures how sharply the curve bends.**
   * When the curve is a straight line, $\kappa = 0$.
   * Where the curve bends most sharply around the corner, $\kappa$ reaches its **maximum peak**.

3. **Finding the Point:**
   The authors define the turning point area ($A_{TP}$) as the exact area where curvature $\kappa(A)$ is maximized:
   $$A_{TP} = \arg\max \kappa(A)$$

The geographic area included up to $A_{TP}$ is declared the **Critical Upwind Basin**.

---

### Step 4: A Concrete Example from the Paper

Take the city of **Sorriso (Mato Grosso, Brazil)**, a major agricultural hub:

* If you traced 100% of the moisture reaching Sorriso, the source area would cover practically the entire continent of South America.
* By calculating the maximum curvature point:
  * The algorithm detects the turning point at an area of about **$1.85 \times 10^6 \text{ km}^2$**.
  * That compact area supplies **~75%** of Sorriso's continental moisture.
  * Adding the remaining millions of square kilometers would only yield the final 25% of moisture.

Thus, conservationists know exactly which $1.85 \times 10^6 \text{ km}^2$ polygon to prioritize for forest preservation.

### 🤖 AREQA
Let’s look strictly at the exact method written in the paper (in the **Methods** section under *"Identification of turning point and critical upwind basin"*).

The authors do not use the standard textbook calculus formula directly on raw data because raw spatial data has noise and arbitrary units. Instead, they follow a strict **4-step algorithm**:

---

### Step 1: Normalize Both Axes to a $[0, 1]$ Unit Square

If area is measured in millions of $\text{km}^2$ and moisture in $\text{mm/yr}$, the shape and slope of the curve depend entirely on the units you choose. 

To remove unit bias, the authors first normalize both axes to run strictly from **0 to 1**:

* **Normalized Area ($x$):**
  $$x = \frac{A - A_{\min}}{A_{\max} - A_{\min}}$$
* **Normalized Cumulative Moisture ($y$):**
  $$y = \frac{F(A) - F_{\min}}{F_{\max} - F_{\min}}$$

Now, the curve starts at $(0, 0)$ and ends at $(1, 1)$.

---

### Step 2: Fit the Data to an Empirical Model

To avoid numerical noise from raw grid cells, they fit the normalized points $(x, y)$ to an empirical continuous function (typically an exponential or power-law saturation curve of the form):

$$y = 1 - e^{-k x} \quad \text{or a similar smooth saturation function}$$

Because this fitted function $y = f(x)$ is smooth and twice differentiable, its derivatives can be computed analytically.

---

### Step 3: Compute the Geometric Curvature $\kappa(x)$

The paper uses the standard mathematical definition of the **curvature of a plane curve**:

$$\kappa(x) = \frac{|y''(x)|}{\left(1 + [y'(x)]^2\right)^{3/2}}$$

Let's break down what each part means:
1. **$y'(x)$ (First derivative):** The marginal contribution. As you add more area, $y'(x)$ decreases because cells are added from highest contributor to lowest.
2. **$y''(x)$ (Second derivative):** The rate at which the marginal return is dropping (the deceleration).
3. **$\kappa(x)$ (Curvature):** Measures the rate of change of the tangent angle per unit arc length. Geometrically, it is the inverse of the radius of the osculating circle ($R = 1/\kappa$) that best fits the curve at that point.

---

### Step 4: Maximize Curvature to Find the "Knee" ($x_{TP}$)

The turning point $x_{TP}$ is defined as the point where curvature reaches its absolute maximum:

$$x_{TP} = \arg\max_{x \in [0, 1]} \kappa(x)$$

To find it analytically:
1. Differentiate $\kappa(x)$ with respect to $x$ and set it to zero:
   $$\frac{d\kappa(x)}{dx} = 0$$
2. Solve for $x$. The root in $[0, 1]$ where $\frac{d^2\kappa}{dx^2} < 0$ gives the normalized turning point $x_{TP}$.
3. Scale $x_{TP}$ back to the original physical area:
   $$A_{TP} = x_{TP} \cdot (A_{\max} - A_{\min}) + A_{\min}$$

Every grid cell that was accumulated before reaching $A_{TP}$ forms the **Critical Upwind Basin**.

---

### A Concrete Numerical Example

Imagine a simplified normalized curve:
$$y = 1 - e^{-3x}$$

1. First derivative: $y' = 3e^{-3x}$
2. Second derivative: $y'' = -9e^{-3x}$
3. Curvature formula:
   $$\kappa(x) = \frac{9e^{-3x}}{\left(1 + 9e^{-6x}\right)^{3/2}}$$

* At $x = 0$: $\kappa(0) = \frac{9}{(1+9)^{3/2}} \approx 0.28$ (relatively flat slope line)
* As $x$ increases: $\kappa(x)$ rises, reaches a maximum peak (around $x \approx 0.25$), and then decays toward $0$ as the curve flattens out.
* The location of that peak ($x \approx 0.25$) is your **turning point**. It means that the first **25%** of the land area captures the bulk of the moisture dynamics, after which marginal gains collapse.
